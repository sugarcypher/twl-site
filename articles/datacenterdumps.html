How Much Land Do Data Centers Actually Use?

Briar Morgan Greenway · ThinkWell Labs

Nobody publishes a straight answer. Campuses hold far more land than their buildings cover, projects get announced and quietly dropped, and the green space that stood there before is rarely counted at all. So I built a tracker that counts it: DataCenterDumps.

Where the sites come from

Every site comes from OpenStreetMap: anything tagged as a data center, plus construction or industrial land whose name says data center in several languages. Status comes from the tags themselves. Construction tags mean under construction; proposed or planned tags mean planned; disused, abandoned, removed or demolished mean abandoned. Each site shows the exact tag that decided its status, so you can check the call.

How land is measured

Footprint is the geodesic area of the mapped outline. A building inside a mapped campus is counted once, under the campus. Sites mapped only as a point have no measured area.

Land share divides footprint by a region's area, using Natural Earth boundaries. Inland lakes are included in the region, so shares run slightly low.

The calculator

The calculator lets you choose which statuses to count and estimate area for point-only sites from the median footprint of their status. It also carries a land-take multiplier of 1×, 7×, 11× or 26×. Those numbers aren't mine: a Missouri Science & Technology Policy Initiative note found three 2026 Missouri campuses held 7, 11 and 26 times their building footprint in total land. You can rank by largest footprint or most green space lost, and export the results as CSV or GeoJSON.

How green space is measured

Green space is read from Sentinel-2 satellite imagery at 10 meters:

  • Keep only the growing season of each year.
  • Drop cloud and shadow pixels.
  • Compute a vegetation index for each pixel.
  • Count pixels at or above 0.3 as green.
  • Multiply that green fraction by the footprint.

"Measure green space now" runs the same method in your browser for any site. A swipe view compares yearly satellite mosaics back to 2016.

What it can't tell you

OpenStreetMap coverage is uneven: good in Europe and North America, sparse elsewhere, and many hyperscale campuses get mapped late or only as points. "Abandoned" depends on mappers updating tags; cancelled projects that never broke ground are rarely mapped. The vegetation index reads living plants, not zoning: lawns count as green, dry grassland may not.

Use it to compare regions and see the pattern. Don't use it to settle a dispute over one parcel.
