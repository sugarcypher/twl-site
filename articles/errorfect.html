What Is a Linguistic Autopsy? Testing One Word

Briar Morgan Greenway · ThinkWell Labs

Some words carry more than their meaning. They carry a standard you never agreed to, installed by someone you never met, working on you every time you use them. A linguistic autopsy is how you find it.

Errorfect is the method. I call it a microscope for connotative and associative attachment: it disrupts a word to reveal the mechanism hidden in the vocabulary.

The protocol

The autopsy runs in order, starting at A, and stops at the first useful revelation:

  • A. Remove the first letter. Stop if it exposes a hidden standard, a sorting rule, a threat, an identity fusion or an unstated metric.
  • B. Remove the first syllable. Stop when the meaning becomes a mechanism, not just a clever fragment.
  • C. Remove the first onset unit, when A and B give only noise.
  • D. Remove a real prefix, only when changing it alters the claim.

Escalation is optional. Going further is more invasive, not more correct.

What it finds

Take perfect. Run A and the standard shows itself: a badge someone pins on you, reversed by the word that gave Errorfect its name. Take normal: a verdict disguised as a description. Take control: strip the mask and you get troll, which is why I swap "control" for "influence."

Some words reveal nothing. Some reveal too much. Both are data.

Three questions after every reveal

  1. What standard does this word assume?
  2. Who authored it?
  3. Do I consent to operate under it now?

Two ways to use it

Word Autopsy. Send one word, with its context if you like. Within 48 hours you get a one-page PDF by email: the protocol run to the first useful revelation, the standard the word carries, any coercive operator inside it, wording that keeps what's true, and a three-question consent checkpoint. $39, one time.

Undertow. For whole texts: an ad, a speech, a memo, a thread, a chapter, or many documents at once. It marks each mechanism in the sentence where it occurs, shows what belief or action it pushes toward, clusters patterns across documents, and flags hidden characters and embedded instructions. $39, one time, with ongoing access.

What it isn't

Errorfect reads language, not people. It describes how words work, not anyone's intent. It's a thinking tool, not a diagnosis, and not a substitute for care.
