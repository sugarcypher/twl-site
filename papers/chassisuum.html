The Chassisuum: Making Enterprises Unownable

Briar M. Greenway and Claude · ThinkWell Labs · October 2026

Abstract

A business can be protected in advance from being bought and dismantled, but not by contract alone. It takes a structure set at founding in which control cannot be bought, the company's essential substance cannot be sold, and debt cannot exceed a published limit. An outside guardian enforces all three.

This paper sets out that structure, which we call the chassisuum. It borrows from trust law, high-yield bond covenants, and admiralty law. It adds a nonprofit guild to hold the vetoes, survey protected companies, and pool their legal defense.

The argument also produced a general finding about measurement. Extraction succeeds partly because accounting cannot see what is taken. Ignorance of that kind comes in three forms (total, chosen, and imposed), and each needs its own remedy.

Everything here is a proposal. Four legal questions require specialist opinions before any of it is used, and nothing in this paper is legal or financial advice.

The problem

The firms that dismantle companies are mostly private equity and leveraged-buyout firms. Their pattern uses a small set of tools, each requiring a decision by whoever controls the company:

  • Debt pushdown: the loan used to buy the company is placed on the company's own balance sheet.
  • Dividend recapitalization: the company borrows more and pays the proceeds to its new owner.
  • Sale-leaseback: the company's real estate is sold, and the company then rents back what it used to own.
  • Management and monitoring fees paid to the owner.
  • Asset stripping before a bankruptcy that the owner's return has already survived.

Mervyn's, Toys "R" Us, and Shopko are the standard case studies. Large asset managers and public pension funds often supply the capital behind buyout funds. Workers' retirement money can therefore finance the buyouts that hollow out workers' own employers.

Why a contract alone cannot forbid it

A contract binds the parties who signed it. Once a buyer owns all the shares, it can amend the charter, replace the board, and waive the restriction. Nobody is left with standing to object.

The usual alternatives are also weak:

  • Charter clauses can be amended by the vote the new owner controls.
  • Post-sale covenants leave the seller only a damages claim after being paid.
  • Public benefit corporations require directors to balance a public purpose against profit, but forbid nothing, and converting out has become easier.
  • Fiduciary duty can push the wrong way. Under Delaware's Revlon doctrine, a board that decides to sell for cash must seek the best price.
  • Courts resist perpetual restraints on selling property, so share-transfer limits in an ordinary corporation must be "reasonable."

Corporate personhood does not help either. A corporation's rights protect it against outsiders, mainly the state, not against its own controlling owner. When that owner strips the company, the act is legally the company's own decision. Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819) shows a charter once protecting a corporation against the state; legislatures then reserved the power to amend charters.

So the design question is not which clause forbids dismantling. It is who, outside the buyer's control, holds a veto, and what that holder can enforce.

The three doors

Every dismantling enters through one of three doors, and leaving any one open defeats the protection.

DoorHow an attacker uses itWhat closes it
Buy the ownerAcquire the controlling sharesVoting control held by a purpose trust, so there is nothing to buy
Buy the assetsHave the board sell the operating business, buildings, and brand, leaving a shellA chassis lock: no sale of core assets without the guardian's veto
Lend until creditors own itLoad the company with debt; control passes to creditors in bankruptcyA leverage cap, restricted payments, and a negative pledge, carried in a permanent covenant bond

Making the company merely unownable closes only the first door. A nonprofit with no owners can still sell everything it has; the nonprofit hospital conversions of the 1990s are the documented example. The precise principle is therefore: control that cannot be owned, assets that cannot be sold, and debt that cannot exceed its limit.

The structure

The protections are set on the company's founding date, its keel day, when no buyer is at the door. They come in six layers.

  1. Founding. The company receives a founding certificate and a permanent identity number, anchored on a public ledger.
  2. Control. A purpose trust holds all voting control and has no beneficiaries who could cash out. Ordinary economic shares stay freely tradable, so investors, founders, and employees can still earn returns. Control and returns are permanently separated, as in Patagonia's 2022 structure.
  3. The chassis. The company's load-bearing substance (its people, skills, relationships, tools, place, and name) sits under a chassis lock. Core operating assets cannot be sold without the guardian's veto.
  4. Debt. A small perpetual bond that can never be called is held by the guild. Its terms carry the anti-extraction covenants: a leverage cap published as a load line, limits on payouts, a ban on sale-leasebacks, reinvestment of any asset-sale proceeds, limits on dealings with insiders, and a negative pledge. Because the bond can never be repaid, the covenants never expire.
  5. Cash flow. A revenue waterfall fixes the order of every dollar: operations and wages, then maintenance, then the reserve, then debt service. Shareholders receive distributions only from what remains.
  6. Time. A mandatory reserve removes the distress-sale moment buyers wait for. Ring-fencing keeps an affiliate's failure from spreading. An anti-redomiciliation covenant blocks moving the company to a state with weaker law.

Inside the walls, directors are fidelity-bonded, insider transactions are disclosed, and workers and the community hold independent board seats. Amendments are possible only by court petition, and only toward the original purpose, following the charitable-trust doctrine of cy-près. That keeps the structure adaptable without opening a loophole.

The working formula: control that cannot be owned, a chassis that cannot be sold, debt that cannot exceed its load line, and an outside guardian watching the people who run it.

Borrowings

The structure is assembled from existing legal and financial machinery, chosen because courts and markets already understand it.

From trust law. Purpose trusts hold property for a purpose rather than for beneficiaries. An enforcer or trust protector supplies the standing to sue that ordinary covenants lack. Directed trusts split trustee roles so no single party can be captured. Delaware and South Dakota both permit trusts of unlimited duration, and South Dakota's statutes are strong on purpose trusts, directed trusts, and trust protectors. Using two states is itself a defense against one legislature changing its law.

From bond finance. High-yield bond indentures already contain covenants that map onto every extraction tool: limits on debt, payouts, sale-leasebacks, asset sales, and dealings with affiliates, plus a change-of-control put. Municipal revenue bonds contribute the waterfall and the debt-service reserve. The Trust Indenture Act of 1939 shows that Congress has already required a professional guardian for one class of long-term promise.

From admiralty and shipping.

One warning from shipping applies directly: the yard that builds a ship and the society that certifies it are different parties. The guild must keep the same separation.

The guild

A covenant without an enforcer is decorative, so the structure needs a standing guardian: a nonprofit guild that stewards the chassisuum. Land trusts are the working template; they hold conservation restrictions in perpetuity, collect a stewardship endowment with each one, and are accredited by an independent commission.

The guild's functions:

  • Hold the vetoes: the keepstone share and the covenant bond for each protected company.
  • Standardize the documents: publish the trust deed, bond indenture, waterfall clause, and chassis lock under a share-alike license.
  • Pool the defense: every protected company pays into a litigation fund, so an attacker faces the whole guild rather than one steward.
  • Fund each stewardship permanently: an endowment collected at founding covers monitoring forever.
  • Survey: annual reviews and a deeper five-year audit, on the classification-society model, with loss of certification as the sanction.
  • Keep the registry: founding documents, load lines, survey results, and every guild vote, hashed onto a public ledger.

The guild must itself be unownable, using the same structure recursively. Its monitoring and its veto should sit with separate bodies chosen by different constituencies, so that capturing one gains an attacker nothing. Surveyors are paid from the endowment, never by the company they survey, and rotate across companies.

Elinor Ostrom's eight design principles for durable commons governance map onto these functions: clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, participation, monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, recognized rights to organize, and nested enterprises.

For an existing portfolio of ventures, the guild and the portfolio's steward must be two separate entities with a firewall between them. A guild that certifies its own founder's companies audits itself.

Threat model

Almost every defense here works by changing the relative cost of attack and defense, not by forbidding anything outright. The two-person rule, used for vaults and weapons handling, is the core principle: no single party can act alone, so capture costs twice.

AttackHow it would workDefense
Litigation attritionOutspend the guild; challenge the trust's validity or recharacterize the bondPooled defense fund; strategically chosen test cases; favorable jurisdictions
Legislative captureLobby a state to weaken purpose-trust or covenant lawRedundancy across several states, not one
Guild captureSeat sympathetic directors; attach conditions to fundingSeparate monitoring and veto bodies; term limits; bar on anyone tied to buyout firms; public votes
ImitationLaunch a weak lookalike certificationTrademarked mark; published standards; verifiable registry
Loan-to-ownBuy a struggling company's debt cheaply, take control in bankruptcyNegative pledge, leverage cap, reserve, non-transferable covenant bond
Buying insidersOffer directors side deals or future jobsFidelity bonds, disclosure, a cooling-off bar on joining acquirers
Counterfeit tracesPlant evidence of mismanagement to trigger a veto or discredit the guildFive defenses, described under Measurement
Narrative attackFrame the structure as anti-marketEconomic shares trade freely; only control and the chassis are protected
Capital starvationConventional lenders refuse covenant-locked companiesMission lenders; reserves that reduce borrowing

Secrecy is not a defense. Kerckhoffs's principle (1883) holds that a system should stay secure even when everything but the key is public. Hidden restrictions often cannot bind a buyer who had no notice of them, and they cannot be adopted by anyone else. The split is the one law enforcement uses: the rules are public, while the guild's monitoring methods and litigation playbook stay private, with a hash of the sealed playbook published to prove it exists unaltered.

The deepest defense is distribution. Many small protected companies, anchored separately in several states, cannot be fought economically one by one.

Measurement

Extraction succeeds partly because the standard instrument cannot see what is taken. "Asset" comes from the Anglo-French asetz, Latin ad satis, "to sufficiency": the property of a dead person sufficient to pay their debts. Accounting still defines an asset by what can be controlled and sold. The chassis falls into accounting's blind spot, and the balance sheet fills it in with goodwill or nothing. A company can be stripped of its most valuable substance without the loss registering until it fails.

An instrument cannot measure its own blind spot. The guild therefore needs a direct measure of the chassis itself, which does not yet exist and must be invented.

How measurement is resisted. Three elements form a causal chain: intent drives concealment; the blind spot is the mechanism, the instrument's filling-in; ignorance is the effect. When the concealer and the ignorant party are the same mind, the result is motivated blindness, which the law treats as knowledge under the doctrine of willful blindness (Global-Tech v. SEB, 2011).

Three kinds of ignorance, three remedies. The division matches Robert Proctor's agnotology, the study of how ignorance is made, which was arrived at independently.

IgnoranceMechanismRemedy in the guild
TotalThe blind spot itselfA direct measure of the chassis
ChosenLooking away when seeing would costSurveyors paid from the endowment, rotated, with cooling-off bars
ImposedConcealment or counterfeiting by an adversaryThe public registry, inference from traces, and the counterfeit defenses

A further hybrid, "undone science" in sociologist David Hess's term, is imposed ignorance disguised as the natural kind: questions never studied because no one with money wanted the answers.

Counterfeit traces. Inference from traces can be reversed to frame a chosen target. Concealment subtracts truth; counterfeiting substitutes a lie. Five defenses apply before any veto rests on inferred wrongdoing:

  1. Require agreement across independent kinds of evidence that are expensive to fake together.
  2. Test each inferred culprit against an explicit "planted" hypothesis, using predictions in channels no one was watching.
  3. Hash every trace onto the ledger when it is collected.
  4. Fix the inference method before seeing whose name it produces.
  5. Report probabilities and ranked alternatives, never bare verdicts.

Build sequence and the orbit

The build runs in three phases, and nothing practical can start until the legal opinions are in hand.

Found

  1. Name the third element in every structure: each working pair needs a named bridge.
  2. Obtain specialist legal opinions on the four open questions.
  3. Choose jurisdictions, at least two, for redundancy.
  4. Begin under a fiscal sponsor, before tax exemption is granted.
  5. Charter the guild with an independent board and mandatory recusal.
  6. Separate the monitoring body from the veto body.
  7. Pay surveyors from the endowment, rotate them, and bar them from later joining surveyed companies or acquirers.

Build

  1. Keel day zero: a first portfolio enters a steward trust.
  2. Place the keepstone with an outside steward.
  3. Issue the perpetual covenant bond.
  4. Install the revenue waterfall.
  5. Publish the load line on the ledger.
  6. Anchor the registry on keel day.
  7. Write the court-petition amendment path.
  8. Develop the chassis instrument.
  9. Put the counterfeit defenses in place before any veto is exercised.
  10. Match a remedy to each kind of ignorance.

Spread

  1. Release the templates under a share-alike license.
  2. Hold a keel day ceremony, making permanence an honor rather than a constraint.
  3. Launch the certification mark.
  4. Serve retiring owners first, before the buyers reach them.
  5. Recruit pension trustees as allies.
  6. Operate the pooled defense.
  7. Pursue policy: a tax incentive for sellers, then model legislation.
  8. Grow many small protected companies rather than a few large ones.

The orbit. A structure without a maintenance cycle decays, which is how the Islamic endowments known as waqf declined. The sustaining cycle encloses everything built: annual surveys, five-year deep audits, the registry kept current, the reserve maintained, steward succession so the keepstone outlives its holders, and red-team reviews to find workarounds before an adversary does. Its three essential motions are watch, pass on, and test.

Open questions and status

Four legal questions decide whether the structure works as designed:

  • Would courts enforce a covenant modeled on the maritime lien, binding unknowing buyers, outside admiralty law?
  • Would a nominal perpetual bond be recharacterized as disguised equity?
  • How do the covenant bond and veto share fare in bankruptcy?
  • Can a share lawfully block a bankruptcy filing? Courts are split.

Strategic questions remain open as well:

  • Does the existing steward-ownership movement, led in the United States by Purpose, see the debt-side layer as a complement or a competitor?
  • Does the guild qualify as a charitable nonprofit under the private-benefit rules?
  • Should the first protected company be the authors' own venture or a willing retiring owner's?

Status of the claims. Legal and historical precedents cited here are well established unless flagged. The structural analogies (maritime law, the two-person rule, triads) are design tools, not legal authority. The physics discussed alongside this work, including white holes, dark matter, and mirror universes, served as analogy and remains open hypothesis; none of the structure depends on it.
