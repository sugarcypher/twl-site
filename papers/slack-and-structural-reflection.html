Slack and Structural Reflection

Briar Morgan Greenway and Claude · ThinkWell Labs · October 2026

Abstract

This paper proposes that the sequence of formal schooling followed by wage work limits people's capacity to notice large institutional patterns, not mainly by controlling what they are taught and not by anyone's intent, but by eliminating slack: unstructured, uncommitted, unscheduled time. Noticing structure is an activity, and it needs time in which nothing else is being demanded.

The paper records how the model was reached, including a correction to the historical premise (the American borrowing was from Prussian schooling, and Horace Mann explicitly rejected its political aims) and a deliberately discarded framing (that schooling was designed to produce compliant workers). It sets out an individual cost model in three stages and three population-scale effects, all stated as theoretical propositions. It then reviews what published research supports. Work on scarcity supports the base mechanism, with replication caveats. Research on time poverty shows that perceived and measured time scarcity come apart, which changes the study design. Research on unemployment, retirement, and idle time tempers the model's central prediction.

The paper closes with a proposed instrument (three separate slack measures and a scored vignette task for structural reflection), a low-cost pilot, kill criteria fixed in advance, the confounds a cross-sectional design cannot solve, and open questions.

The claim

Greenway. The pattern of formal schooling followed by wage work does not primarily suppress reflection by controlling what people are taught, nor by any intent to produce compliant workers. It suppresses reflection by eliminating slack.

Noticing large structural patterns is an activity. It requires time in which nothing else is being demanded. Remove that time and the noticing does not happen, regardless of what the person knows, believes, or has read. No censorship is required. No intent is required. No one has to be hiding anything.

The claim's strength is that it is substrate-independent. It does not depend on schooling remaining as it is, on any particular ideology holding, or on the content of the work. Whatever the next stage of the system rewards, it still consumes the hours. Exhaustion and fragmentation do the work that suppression would otherwise have to do.

How the model was reached

The path matters because two of its steps were corrections and one was a discarded framing that could otherwise creep back in.

1. The structural borrowing, corrected. An early statement in the conversation named Russia as the source of the American school model. The borrowing was Prussian, and it was selective. Horace Mann, secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education, sailed for Europe on 1 May 1843 at his own expense. He visited schools in England, Ireland, and Scotland; in Prussia (including Berlin, Potsdam, Halle, and the Rhenish provinces), Saxony, and several smaller German states; and in Holland, Belgium, and France. He reported on the tour in his Seventh Annual Report (1844).

What Mann praised in that report was specific. He called the classification of pupils by age and attainment "the first element of superiority in a Prussian school," and he praised the quality and training of Prussian teachers, their methods of instruction, and the rarity of corporal punishment in practice. He described compulsory attendance favorably and argued that it was not a prerogative of despotism. Some of these causes predate the tour: Massachusetts opened the first state normal school for training teachers in Lexington in July 1839, with Mann as its leading advocate. Massachusetts passed the first American compulsory attendance law in 1852, after Mann had left the board.

2. The intent framing, discarded. The familiar claim that this structure was designed to produce obedient, uncreative workers is a retrospective reading associated most strongly with John Taylor Gatto, notably in the chapter "The Prussian Connection" in The Underground History of American Education (2000) and in his essay "Against School" (Harper's, 2003). It is not Mann's stated case. Mann's report draws an explicit line: Americans could copy the Prussian schoolmaster's methods "without adopting his notions of passive obedience to government." His public argument was democratic; in his Twelfth Annual Report (1848) he called education "the great equalizer of the conditions of men." Prussian aims were themselves different from factory discipline: historians of eighteenth-century Prussian schooling describe it as an instrument of religious and social discipline in the service of the absolutist state (Melton, 1988).

This framing is discarded, and its discarding is load-bearing. The model does not need it and is weaker with it.

3. Emergent design, retained. Greenway. What replaces intent is selection pressure. Structures that reproduce themselves are the ones whose outputs fit the next stage. Schooling produces people who staff the institutions that fund and shape schooling, and each round tightens the fit. Nobody designs it; the fit is selected for. This converts the claim from a historical assertion about motives, which is hard to test and easy to argue about, into a claim about mechanism and effect, which can be measured.

4. The Orwell inversion. Greenway. Where Nineteen Eighty-Four is assigned in school, it is read inside the very system it describes. The proposed function is not warning but inoculation: the book is taught as literature, analyzed for irony and technique, resolved in a five-paragraph essay, and filed under dystopia, fictional, averted. The alarm is discharged in advance. The vocabulary is supplied without the exit.

Claude-constructed. The specific mechanism is that the assignment format performs the neutralization, because anything answerable within a fixed essay form is thereby presented as having an answer. The published literature bearing on this reading is reviewed below; no study has tested it directly.

5. Why slack, and not the alternatives. Three candidate accounts of where the reproductive loop breaks were considered:

  1. Instability. The pressure selects for fit only while the next stage stays stable; change what work rewards and the loop loses its grip.
  2. Leakage. The loop requires that its outputs not notice, yet people do notice. Either the training is leakier than the model implies, or noticing was never the constraint and acting was.
  3. Vestigiality. The loop does not break; some other pressure becomes binding and schooling continues without doing the work.

Greenway. A fourth account supersedes them: slack. It explains why leakage is not a counterexample (people with unstructured hours notice, and they are the ones who do), and it makes instability and vestigiality irrelevant, because whatever the next stage is, it still consumes the time. It also collapses the twelve school years and the working life into a single mechanism rather than two: bell schedule, then shift schedule. The content differs; the fragmentation does not.

The individual cost model

Claude-constructed from Greenway's premise. The following are theoretical propositions, not findings. Each is stated so that it could be operationalized.

Proposition 1. Horizon contraction. What goes first is not happiness but the sense of authorship: the felt experience that one's life is being composed rather than responded to. Planning horizons contract to the pay period, then the weekend, then the evening. This is not chosen; it is what fits the available attention.

Proposition 2. Identity migrates outward. Where authorship is unavailable, identity is borrowed. The person locates themselves by role, consumption, and affiliation, using prefabricated material. This is not weakness. It is cheaper than construction, and it fits the time available.

Proposition 3. Atrophy, and defense of the cause. The capacity itself degrades. Given an unexpected free day, the person does not reflect; they cannot find the thread. Unstructured time becomes aversive rather than valuable, so it is filled deliberately. At this point the person begins to defend the fragmentation that produced the state: busyness becomes preference, then virtue.

Proposition 3 closes the trap from the inside, and it is the least supported of the three. It is the first thing the instrument should try to break.

Population-scale effects

Claude-constructed. These are stated as further propositions. The claim is that they do not merely aggregate but compound.

Proposition 4. Assembly failure. Reflection of the relevant kind is not solitary. It requires other people with unscheduled time to reflect with. If slack is scarce across a population, the noticing that would have to be collective cannot assemble, even where individuals manage it.

Proposition 5. Variance narrowing. Prefabricated identity is supplied by whoever is selling. What fills the gap left by Proposition 2 is whatever is cheapest to distribute. The result is convergence without coordination: many people arriving at similar places because the same few options were on offer.

Proposition 6. Horizontal enforcement. If unstructured time is individually aversive, its collective expression is a culture that treats busyness as virtue and reflection as indulgence. The pressure is then applied by colleagues, family, and peers rather than by institutions. At that point the loop no longer requires schooling at all; it is self-maintaining in what people expect of each other.

What the literature supports

Sourced. This section covers the base mechanism, the measurement of slack, and the evidence on groups the model predicts should reflect more. Several findings temper the model, and they are reported as such.

Scarcity consumes cognitive bandwidth. Mullainathan and Shafir's Scarcity (2013) argues that a shortage of any resource (money, time, calories, social ties) captures attention and reduces the "bandwidth" left for other demands. In laboratory games, Shah, Mullainathan and Shafir (2012) found that scarcity focused people on immediate problems, led them to neglect others, and encouraged overborrowing. Mani and colleagues (2013) found that prompting lower-income shoppers in New Jersey to think about a large financial expense lowered their performance on cognitive tests, and that Indian sugarcane farmers scored better after harvest than before it. Princeton's summary of the work, written by Morgan Kelly, defines scarcity as "any deficit" and compares the measured effect to roughly 13 IQ points. The two 2013 studies tested financial scarcity, however; the case for time scarcity rests mainly on the book and the 2012 laboratory games.

The base mechanism is not settled. Carvalho, Meier and Wang (2016) found little evidence that cognitive function differed before and after payday among low-income U.S. households. An audit of the wider scarcity literature (O'Donnell et al., 2021), which re-ran 20 studies, found considerable variability, with some strong replications and some clear failures. The scarcity work supports Proposition 1 and the base mechanism as a plausible pathway. It does not reach Propositions 2 or 3, and it does not address the population effects.

Perceived and measured time scarcity diverge. Time-use researchers define objective discretionary time as the time left after what is necessary for paid work, unpaid household work, and personal care (Goodin et al., 2008). Time poverty as a feeling is measured separately, and the two come apart. Hamermesh and Lee (2007) found that people in higher-earning households report more time stress than others who spend the same hours on paid and household work. Mogilner, Chance and Norton (2012) found that spending time helping others made people feel they had more time than spending it on themselves or receiving a windfall of free time. Giurge, Whillans and West (2020) define time poverty as the feeling of having too many things to do and not enough time, and link it to lower well-being, health, and productivity. In a seven-day diary study, Dong and Sun (2025) found that subjective time poverty predicted well-being more strongly than the objective amount of discretionary time.

Consequence for the model. The instrument must measure perceived and measured slack separately and treat which one predicts reflection as an empirical question. If perceived slack predicts and measured hours do not, the model survives but changes character: the intervention target becomes the experience of time, not its quantity. That is a different and less comfortable conclusion.

Contiguous versus fragmented free time. Time-use research treats the quality of free time, not just its quantity, as measurable. Women's free time in particular has been found to be more fragmented into short episodes and more often combined with unpaid work or child care (Bittman and Wajcman, 2000; Mattingly and Bianchi, 2003). Dong and Sun (2025) found that fragmentation of time use predicted feeling time-poor. Research on task switching shows that attention to an interrupted task lingers and degrades performance on the next (Leroy, 2009), which makes it plausible that fragmented hours deliver less usable attention than the same hours in one block. No study located for this paper tests whether contiguous free time predicts structural or reflective thinking. The contiguity proposition is therefore untested, and the instrument is designed to test it.

Unemployment: time without slack. The classic study of an unemployed community, Jahoda, Lazarsfeld and Zeisel's Marienthal (first published in German in 1933), describes a "weary community" in which mass unemployment produced apathy and a loss of time structure, not reflection or political activity; borrowing from the workers' library declined. Jahoda (1981) later formalized this as the latent deprivation model: employment provides, besides income, time structure, social contact, collective purpose, status, and regular activity. A 2023 meta-analysis found that employed people report more of all five than unemployed people (Paul et al., 2023), and unemployment reliably harms mental health (Paul and Moser, 2009). This cuts against the naive form of the model's prediction. Involuntary unemployment brings unscheduled hours together with financial scarcity and the loss of structure and purpose, and the scarcity literature itself predicts that financial worry consumes bandwidth. Hours freed by deprivation are not the uncommitted time the model calls slack.

Retirement and cognitive engagement. Retirement provides a large, roughly exogenous increase in discretionary time. Studies using pension eligibility ages to isolate its effect find that retirement is followed by modest declines in memory (Rohwedder and Willis, 2010; Bonsang, Adam and Perelman, 2012), though estimates vary and some find no effect for white-collar workers and possible benefits for blue-collar workers (Coe et al., 2012). An experiment with older adults found that sustained engagement in demanding new activities improved memory, while less demanding social activities did not (Park et al., 2014). The pattern suggests that what matters is what time is used for, not its amount alone. Memory is not structural reflection, so these findings do not test the model, but they caution against assuming retirees will reflect more.

Idle time can be aversive. In a series of studies, many participants found 6 to 15 minutes alone with their thoughts unpleasant, and in one study 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women gave themselves at least one mild electric shock rather than sit with nothing to do (Wilson et al., 2014). Across two large U.S. datasets, Sharif, Mogilner and Hershfield (2021) found that well-being rises with discretionary time only up to a point and then falls, and that the decline is reduced when the time is spent on productive activities. Both findings bear on Proposition 3. Aversion to unstructured time exists, but it may be a general baseline rather than a product of fragmentation. Proposition 3 therefore needs to show that the aversion varies with a person's history of fragmentation, not merely that it exists.

Narrative persuasion and inoculation. In persuasion research, inoculation has a precise meaning: exposing people to a weakened version of an argument, together with its refutation, makes them more resistant to a stronger version later (McGuire and Papageorgis, 1961; McGuire, 1964). Greenway's reading of the classroom Nineteen Eighty-Four fits this structure closely: the critique is delivered in weakened form with its resolution attached, which would predict resistance to later, stronger encounters with the same critique. Narrative transportation research adds a possible mechanism. Readers who are absorbed in a story counterargue less and adopt more story-consistent beliefs, and labeling a story as fiction rather than fact did not reduce this effect (Green and Brock, 2000; see also Moyer-Gusé, 2008). Persuasive effects of fiction can even grow over time (Appel and Richter, 2007). An analytic classroom treatment that keeps readers out of the story could reduce its effect.

The evidence also points the other way. Jones and Paris (2018) found in three experiments that exposure to dystopian narratives increased willingness to justify radical, including violent, political action. Dystopian fiction is not inert, and labeling it as fiction does not neutralize it. If the inoculation reading holds, it must depend on the school frame and the assignment format, which is exactly the part that has not been tested.

The instrument

Greenway's design direction; Claude's construction.

What it tests. The prediction worth testing cuts against the intuitive one. If education governs structural reflection, more schooling should mean more of it. If slack governs it, then people with real slack (some retired, underemployed, and independently working people) should out-reflect credentialed professionals working long weeks. The literature above adds a qualification: slack must be distinguished from time freed by deprivation, so financial strain is measured and controlled.

Formally: structural reflection is predicted by discretionary time, controlling for educational attainment, and the slack measures outperform education.

Measuring slack. Three measures, deliberately kept separate.

  1. Measured discretionary time. A recall diary for two weekdays and one weekend day. Established surveys differ in format. The American Time Use Survey asks respondents, by telephone, to walk through the previous day from 4 a.m., recording each activity and how long it lasted (Bureau of Labor Statistics). The Harmonised European Time Use Surveys use a diary of 144 ten-minute slots for one weekday and one weekend day (Eurostat, 2019). The Day Reconstruction Method has respondents reconstruct the previous day as a sequence of episodes (Kahneman et al., 2004). This instrument uses an episode-based recall diary with start and end times, so that block lengths can be computed directly. Discretionary hours are waking hours not committed to paid work, commuting, caregiving, domestic necessity, or personal care (following Goodin et al., 2008).
  2. Contiguity. The longest uninterrupted discretionary block, and the number of discretionary blocks per day. The model claims reflection needs contiguous time, not aggregate minutes. Four hours in eight fragments and four hours in one block are the same on the first measure and different on the model's own logic. If contiguity does not outperform total hours, one specific version of the model is wrong.
  3. Perceived time scarcity. The Chronic Time Pressure Inventory (Denovan and Dagnall, 2019), a 13-item self-report scale covering feeling harried and awareness of time shortage, with published psychometric evaluation (Denovan et al., 2023). The Perceived Time Poverty Scale (Zheng and Zhang, 2021) is an alternative. This measure is never collapsed into the first.

Measuring structural reflection. Four to six short institutional vignettes, each describing an arrangement, a procedure, or a recurring outcome neutrally in under 120 words, in domains varied enough that no single occupational background confers an advantage. The prompt for each is: why is it like this, and why does it stay like this? Responses are open-ended, spoken or written, and untimed within reason. What is scored is the level at which the person locates causation, and whether they account for persistence.

Each vignette receives a composite score, and the person's score is the mean across vignettes.

Scoring procedure. Two raters, blind to every predictor: no time-use data, no education, no occupation. Inter-rater reliability is reported before any analysis. The rubric, with worked examples at each level, is pre-registered and fixed before data collection.

The critical design constraint. This must not become an intelligence or verbal-fluency test. Raters score the level of attribution, never the eloquence. Response length is recorded and checked as a possible confound. A vocabulary measure and educational attainment are both controlled. If the reflection score correlates more strongly with verbal ability than with any slack measure, the instrument is measuring the wrong thing and should be rebuilt or abandoned.

Analysis. Regress the composite reflection score on each slack measure (total discretionary hours, longest contiguous block, perceived scarcity), controlling for educational attainment, verbal ability, income and financial strain, occupational category, age, and caregiving load. Compare model fit against a specification using education alone. Report the null. If it is null, that is the finding.

The pilot

Everything above describes a full study. The realistic first step is a pilot of the reflection rubric alone: twenty to thirty responses scored by two raters. The pilot asks three questions. Are the attribution levels distinguishable in practice, as shown by inter-rater agreement? Does verbal ability swamp everything else? Do the vignettes favor any occupational background?

Vignettes should be written by someone blind to the hypothesis, and the agreement threshold for proceeding should be set in the pre-registration. The pilot costs little and can kill the instrument early, which is its purpose. If the rubric survives, the slack measures are added and the full study proceeds.

Kill criteria

These are set before anything is collected. The model is wrong, or wrong in the form stated, if:

  • Educational attainment predicts the reflection score and no slack measure adds explanatory power over it.
  • Both perceived and measured slack are null after controls.
  • The reflection score loads more heavily on verbal ability than on any slack measure.
  • Contiguity and total discretionary hours are indistinguishable and neither predicts, so the contiguity refinement fails along with the base claim.
  • Proposition 3's specific prediction fails: people with high measured slack and low perceived slack do not show the aversion-to-unstructured-time pattern.

Confounds

Reverse causation. People who reflect may arrange their lives to obtain slack. A cross-sectional design cannot separate this from slack producing reflection, and it is the most likely way an apparently positive result would be meaningless.

Selection. Independently working people chose that arrangement. Retired people did not choose retirement in the same sense, and unemployed people often did not choose at all. These groups differ in more than slack, including income security, structure, and purpose, as the unemployment and retirement literature shows.

Exogenous variation in time is the only real answer. Imposed schedule changes supply it: four-day-week trials, furloughs, mandated shift restructuring, and involuntary reduced hours. A longitudinal design built around one of these is worth more than a large cross-sectional sample, and it is the next step if the pilot and first study are not null. Changes that reduce income as well as hours should be avoided or modeled, since financial scarcity is itself a predicted drain on bandwidth.

Open questions

These are the objections that need answers before the full study is run.

  • The reflection measure may be circular. The rubric rewards structural explanation, and the model is a structural explanation. An instrument that scores agreement with the model highest is a live risk, and vignette selection is where it would enter. Who writes the vignettes, and are they blind to the hypothesis?
  • Is "reflection" the right construct? The model treats structural noticing as a good and its absence as a cost. That is an evaluative commitment beneath what is presented as a descriptive model. It should be stated as such or defended.
  • The novelty lives in Propositions 2 and 3. Proposition 1 is close to a restatement of established scarcity findings. If the study confirms only Proposition 1, it has confirmed something already known. The atrophy and defense claims carry the novelty and are the hardest to measure.
  • The Orwell inversion needs a testable prediction. It is not yet connected to the instrument. One candidate: among people who read Nineteen Eighty-Four or similar dystopian fiction, reading it as a school assignment predicts lower structural reflection than reading it voluntarily, controlling for reading generally. Inoculation theory and the Jones and Paris results make this a sharp test. Otherwise it belongs in a separate piece.
  • The population effects are untestable as stated. Assembly failure, variance narrowing, and horizontal enforcement need historical time-use data compared against a measure of collective structural discourse, or they remain commentary.
  • What counts as slack? The unemployment, retirement, and idle-time findings show that unscheduled hours alone do not produce engagement. The model needs a definition of slack that excludes time freed by deprivation, without making the definition circular (slack defined as whatever time produces reflection).

Provenance

This paper began in a conversation between Briar Morgan Greenway and Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, recorded in a working document dated 3 September 2026. Claude verified its factual claims and added the published literature in October 2026. Three kinds of material are combined here, and the argument's strength differs sharply between them.

Greenway: the central claim; slack as the binding constraint and its supersession of the instability, leakage, and vestigiality accounts; emergent design by selection pressure; the single mechanism joining the bell schedule and the shift schedule; the inoculation reading of Nineteen Eighty-Four; the decision to discard the intent framing; and the design direction for the instrument.

Claude-constructed: the three-stage individual cost model (Propositions 1 to 3); the three population-scale effects (Propositions 4 to 6); the assignment-format mechanism for the Orwell inversion; the instrument's construction, including the contiguity measure, the scoring rubric, the analysis plan, the kill criteria, and the pilot; and the mapping of the Orwell inversion onto inoculation theory.

Sourced: the history of Mann's tour, his reports, and the Massachusetts dates; the description of Gatto's thesis; and all findings in the literature section, each cited at the point of use.

The partition exists because a model built quickly, in one sitting, that explains a great deal, is in exactly the condition where felt coherence outruns evidence. Readers, including later versions of the authors, should be able to see which parts were earned. The propositions are hypotheses for the study to test, and the parts most likely to be wrong are Propositions 2 to 6.
