The Path to the Trustee Commonwealth†

A transition plan for adopting the Trustee Commonwealth of Earth: by consent, in stages, with the checks and balances working before the institutions they protect exist.

Companion to The Trustee Commonwealth of Earth. Design proposal, provisional until ratified. Drafted by Claude (Anthropic) on direction, 22 October 2026. Names marked † are Claude coinages, in play unless struck. Years are planning estimates, not forecasts. Version 2 adds the great-power strategy (section 7). Partner organizations in section 8 were checked as active in this session; other precedents are public record cited from knowledge.

1. Why the transition is the most dangerous part

Every structure loses its protections at the moment it's being built. The people building it hold power that the finished design would deny them, and there is no court, chamber or audit yet to stop them. Revolutions that promised liberty and delivered terror failed in exactly this window. So the plan applies checks and balances to itself before the Commonwealth exists. Those checks are stricter than the finished design's, because the transition has fewer of them to rely on.

2. Founding rules that bind the transition itself

  1. Consent only. Polities join only through their own lawful constitutional process and with their people's consent. No coercion, no violence, no armed wing, ever. Any participant who uses force, bribery or intimidation is expelled and referred to the courts.
  2. Four powers, four bodies. Drafting, ratifying, operating and auditing are held by four separate bodies. No person sits in two of them.
  3. Builders don't rule. Anyone who drafts the charter is barred from holding its offices for ten years. They may advise, without a vote. (Precedent: the French National Constituent Assembly's self-denying ordinance of 1791. It also produced an inexperienced legislature, which is why drafters keep an advisory role here.)
  4. Every transitional body dies on schedule. Its sunset date is written into its founding text. Only the ratifying body can extend it, once, for at most two years. Acts taken after the sunset are void.
  5. Gates, not dates. A phase advances only when an independent audit certifies success criteria that were published in advance. A failed gate means redesign. Twice failed means the module is dropped.
  6. No single owner. No donor may supply more than 2% of annual funds, and no polity more than 15% of a transitional budget. The books are open monthly.
  7. The mirror test at every stage. Every founding body includes members from opposing political camps and from every inhabited continent. A clause advances only if people who disagree with its authors can accept it.
  8. Withdrawal at every phase. Any city, region or polity can leave a pilot, compact or treaty under terms written in advance.

4. Five phases, five gates

Phase 0: Seed

Years 0 to 3 · ends at Gate A
Goal
A charter worth adopting, and early evidence that its parts work at small scale.
Actions
  • Publish the charter under an open license so anyone can copy, test or fork it.
  • Form a Founding Trust† as a nonprofit. A third of its board is chosen by lot, a third are scientists, and a third come from opposing political camps.
  • Build a public prototype of the Office of Measurement: a planetary-boundary and social-floor dashboard drawn from open satellite and statistical data.
  • Run mirror-test review panels on every inhabited continent.
  • Run three or more citizens' assembly pilots, online and local, chosen by verifiable lottery.
Checks
Founders are barred from future charter office. The 2% donor cap and open books apply from the first dollar. Every pilot is evaluated by someone who didn't build it.
Gate A
Mirror panels on five or more continents, including opposing camps, accept at least two-thirds of the clauses, and the rest are redrafted. An independent lab reproduces the measurement prototype. Three assembly pilots are completed and evaluated.
Kill criteria
If the panels reject the core after three drafting rounds, the structure is wrong. Redesign from the Ordering Clause up.

Phase 1: Modules

Years 2 to 10 · ends at Gate B
Goal
Each organ of the charter proven in real jurisdictions, with no treaty needed.
Actions
  • Measurement compact among willing states and regions: shared independent measurement with satellite cross-checks.
  • Permanent citizens' assemblies in cities and regions.
  • Commons dividend pilots: resource rent or carbon fees returned to residents in equal shares.
  • Commons Advocate pilots inside attorney general or ombudsman offices.
  • Future-generations commissioners on the Welsh model.
  • Thriving accounts published alongside gross domestic product in willing jurisdictions. Gross domestic product is then removed from their binding rules once the accounts have run for three years.
  • Gate-test pilots: free water lifelines, open access to publicly funded research, and prize funds for essential medicines.
  • Repair-justice and healing partnerships, led by the Indigenous nations and communities that choose to run them: repair circles alongside the courts, and traditional healers working with clinics, under benefit-sharing agreements.
  • Ranch-conversion pilots: willing ranchers paid to convert land to pollinator sanctuary garden farms, native rewilding, agrivoltaics and humane insect farms, with yields, pollinator counts and rancher incomes scored, as the first step toward converting one-third of ranch land.
  • Provisioning pilots: cancelling medical and student debt, on the model of nonprofits that buy medical debt cheaply and cancel it; time banks for care; fading local currencies on the Chiemgauer model; and provisioning cooperatives taking over one floor sector at a time until it has no price.
  • Daily-foundation pilots: crisis care teams in place of police for mental health calls, universal fresh school meals sourced from local farms, and right-to-disconnect and shorter-workweek trials, each scored on mental health, diet quality and chronic stress.
  • Neurochemical freedom pilots: decriminalization only where drug checking, treatment on demand and safe supply are already running, following Portugal's model (2001) and learning from Oregon's reversal (2024).
  • Contribution Commons Fund pilots: creators who release art, music, tools or knowledge into the commons are paid by use and by how much others build on it, following the public lending right model.
  • Guardianship pilots for rivers and forests on the Whanganui model, plus an evidence register on animal sentience.
  • A license club: jurisdictions coordinating "safe before sale" rules, starting with compulsive digital design.
Likely early hosts
Cities and regions already running assemblies; small, climate-exposed states such as the Pacific island nations that pushed for the International Court of Justice's 2025 climate opinion; governments already running wellbeing budgets. These are inferences from track records, not commitments.
Checks
Every pilot publishes its success criteria before it starts. Pilots run under the host's existing law and courts, and any host can end its pilot.
Gate B
At least six of the nine organs piloted in 20 or more jurisdictions on four or more continents. At least two-thirds meet their published criteria in independent evaluation. No capture tripwire left unresolved.
Kill criteria
An organ that fails evaluation through two redesign cycles is dropped or replaced, and the charter is amended.

Phase 2: Founding Compact†

Years 8 to 18 · ends at Gate C
Goal
A binding treaty among founding polities that creates the first planetary organs, with narrow, listed powers.
Actions
  • Negotiate the Compact. It contains the Ordering Clause, the Office of Measurement, a Commons Trust over the commons members agree to share, the Commons Advocate, a Court of Rights open to persons in member polities, and all three chambers: the House of Lots, the House of Polities and the Steward Chamber.
  • Members apply border charges to non-member imports equal to their embedded ecological cost. Precedent: the Montreal Protocol's trade restrictions against non-parties, which helped bring holdouts in.
Entry into force
30 polities representing at least 15% of the world's population, from four or more continents, including at least 8 low-income and 8 high-income polities. Precedent for threshold triggers: the Paris Agreement and the High Seas Treaty.
Checks
Each member ratifies by its own constitutional process. The Compact expires in 20 years unless re-ratified, and any member may withdraw on two years' notice. The House of Lots starts in an advisory role and gains blocking power only after an independent review finds it deliberates well.
Gate C
The Compact is in force, and observers from non-member polities certify the first lottery and the first elections.
Kill criteria
If the threshold isn't reached within ten years of opening for signature, cut the scope and renegotiate.

Phase 3: Expansion

Years 15 to 35 · ends at Gate D
Goal
Membership grows until staying out costs more than joining.
Actions
  • Members get the dividend, market access, shared technology and shared disaster plans.
  • Non-members face border charges.
  • Compact powers expand one listed step at a time as membership crosses set thresholds.
  • Seek a United Nations Charter review conference under Article 109 to align or integrate existing institutions.
  • Hold the transition jubilee in member polities: household medical, student and consumer debt and mortgages on main homes are cancelled, and the debts of formerly colonized polities are written off. Savings begin converting to exchange credits on a published schedule.
Checks
The power ratchet runs both ways† NEW: if membership falls back below a threshold, the powers tied to it retract automatically. Spending is capped as a share of members' combined output. The Steward veto-rate and regional-trust tripwires stay live. No planetary army, at any stage.
Gate D
Members represent at least half the world's population and a majority of polities. An independent audit shows members' floor coverage rising and ecological trends bending faster than in comparable non-members.
Kill criteria
If members don't outperform comparable non-members after ten years, stop expanding and redesign.

Phase 4: Planetary Convention

Years 30 to 50 · ends at Gate E
Goal
The full charter, drafted by the people of Earth and ratified by them.
Actions
A Planetary Constitutional Convention drafts the final charter. It sits in thirds: people chosen by worldwide lottery, polity delegates, and stewards (scientists, Indigenous nations, and people under 30). It drafts the final charter from the Compact's track record. It runs the mirror test and submits the draft for ratification.
Ratification
A triple test: two-thirds of polities; polities representing at least 60% of the world's population; and a planetary referendum carried in at least five of the six inhabited continents. The referendum ballot offers three options by ranked vote: ratify, return for amendment, or reject.
Checks
Convention members are barred from charter office for ten years, and the convention dissolves the day it submits its draft. International observers certify the vote. Any polity may decline and remain outside on the exit terms.
Gate E
Ratification succeeds. Every interim body dissolves on a fixed date, and the first full lotteries and elections are observed and certified.
Kill criteria
If ratification fails twice, the Compact continues as it is, and no new attempt is allowed for 15 years.

Phase 5: Living charter

Ongoing
Rule
Re-ratification every 25 years. A trust tripwire can trigger an early convention. The charter can lose, and it has to be able to.

6. What blocks the path, and how the plan routes around it

United Nations Charter amendment

Amendments need ratification by all five permanent Security Council members (Article 108), so a reformed United Nations can't be the starting point. The plan builds outside the United Nations by treaty among willing polities, then seeks an Article 109 review conference once the Compact has a track record.

Great powers

The United States, China, India and Russia are unlikely early members. The plan is built to work without them for decades. Border charges and membership benefits change the math over time, as the Montreal Protocol's trade measures did.

National constitutions

Some forbid transferring sovereignty without a referendum. The plan goes through each country's own process. Sovereignty is pooled, as in the European Union, never seized.

Fear of "world government"

This fear is reasonable, and the design answers it in structure, not in messaging: a thin planetary layer, nations that persist, no army, a real right of exit, powers that retract, and a dividend people can feel.

Incumbent industries

The license club starts in sectors where public coalitions already exist, such as children's digital safety and dangerous chemicals. It reaches the heaviest polluting sectors through Commons Trust fees once the Compact is in force.

Time

Fifty years is longer than most careers. The plan survives its founders by design: open license, a rotating Founding Trust, and institutions that outlast any one person.

7. Keeping great powers from interfering

No plan can make interference impossible. A nuclear-armed power can crush a small member, and no charter changes that. What the plan can do is make interference unnecessary, unrewarding, costly and visible. Great powers interfere when a project looks more expensive to tolerate than to stop. Every measure below works on one side of that equation.

History shows both sides of the pattern. Rival great powers have accepted shared regimes when the regime ended a contest neither could win cheaply and gave each side access or predictability. The Antarctic Treaty (1959) demilitarized a continent at the height of the Cold War. The Outer Space Treaty (1967) followed. The Montreal Protocol (1987) won over both superpowers once substitutes for ozone-destroying chemicals existed. Great powers interfere when a project looks like a rival bloc or a threat to their rule: the Cold War coups in Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954) and Chile (1973), and the Soviet invasions of Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968).

1. Don't look like a threat

No army, no alliance, no mutual-defense pact. Members keep their existing defense and alliances. The Commonwealth never funds opposition movements in non-member countries. Great powers get observer seats at every session from the first day. Growth starts with technical, low-profile organs like measurement and citizens' assemblies. Political scientist David Mitrany (1943) argued that cooperation built through technical functions can grow where political unions provoke resistance.

2. Give them a stake: the Commons Partner tier†

Any polity can join the measurement compact, the Commons Trust and the license club as a partner, without accepting the liberties requirement. Partners get market access and a population-based share of commons rents. They don't get seats in any chamber.

The trade-off is stated openly: this lets rivals in without endorsing their governments, at some cost to principle.

Interests each power can see (my inference): stable climate and supply chains for everyone; a large rules-based market for clean-technology producers; per-person dividends and historic-responsibility rents that favor populous developing powers; a funded transition window for fossil exporters.
Precedent: the European Economic Area, which brings countries into the single market without full European Union membership.

3. Remove the prize

A government that takes power by coup, or by foreign-backed overthrow, is not recognized. Its seats are suspended, and the commons revenue owed to that polity's people is held in escrow in each person's name. It is released to the people, never to the regime. No polity holds a veto, unlike the five permanent members of the United Nations Security Council, and seat and budget caps apply. Capturing one member, even a large one, buys very little.
Precedent: the African Union's suspension of members after coups; the Inter-American Democratic Charter (2001).

4. Make interference costly, without weapons

Economic coercion or attack against any member triggers pre-agreed, automatic trade, license and commons measures by every member. Members are drawn from every bloc, so any power that coerces one of them hurts its own partners. Great powers' own companies hold planetary licenses, so those companies share the cost.

Escrowed dividends† NEW: with a non-member polity's consent, the Commons Trust holds dividends in its people's names. That builds a constituency for the project inside every great power. The risk is real: this can itself look like interference. It is done only openly, never covertly, and released only with the polity's consent.
Precedent: the European Union's Anti-Coercion Instrument (2023).

5. Leave nothing to capture or decapitate

Lottery works as counterintelligence. A random, verifiable draw can't be targeted in advance: you can't plant an agent in a seat no one can predict. Venice used rounds of lot in choosing its doge from 1268 for exactly this reason, to defeat faction capture.

Short terms, rotation, disclosure of foreign contacts, and financial monitoring during and after service. Each organ is seated in three polities across rival blocs, with plans for continuing if any host is coerced. Records are copied across jurisdictions. Measurement labs sit in rival blocs, and the two-of-three rule means no single power can falsify a finding. Satellite data is checked across independent systems, such as the American Landsat and European Copernicus satellites. Several parties contribute the randomness for each lottery. Votes have paper backups.

6. Make interference visible: the Interference Register†

Documented interference attempts are published with their evidence. Publication happens only after a citizen audit jury reviews the attribution, so that false accusations can't themselves be used as propaganda. All deliberation is public and recorded. Known disinformation lines are answered publicly before they spread. Exposure raises the reputational price of interfering.

Switzerland and the Antarctic Treaty have survived great powers by being more useful to tolerate than worth the cost of destroying, and by offering no single target. If the two largest powers decide at the same moment that the project is a threat, it will likely fail. The sequencing is built to avoid that moment: stay technical, stay open, and become useful before becoming large.

8. The first 24 months: what a small lab can start

  1. Publish the charter and this plan under an open license, with the provenance record attached.
  2. Commission the first full costing of the floor, polity by polity, to replace the charter's order-of-magnitude estimates.
  3. Translate the plain-language version, first into the ten most widely spoken languages, then with Indigenous language partners. Commission artists, musicians and storytellers through an early Contribution Commons Fund, because people come to want a future before they vote for one.
  4. Build the Office of Measurement prototype from open data. It is the most concrete and fundable piece, and it's useful even if nothing else is adopted.
  5. Recruit the first mirror-test panels through existing citizens' assembly networks.
  6. Incorporate the Founding Trust with the three-way board, the donor cap and the self-denying rule written into its bylaws before any money arrives.
  7. Approach the partners below, starting with practitioners, not funders: evidence first, money second.
